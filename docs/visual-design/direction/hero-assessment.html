Design decision record · 28 October 2026 · Homepage hero

Superseded implementation decision: the follow-up build-and-test comparison implements a wide photographic overlay with separate copy on narrower screens. This document records the earlier split baseline and benchmark research. Its numerical alternatives scores are historical judgments, not measured outcomes.

Keep the human story. Bring care within reach.

Recommendation implemented: a static split hero with direct doctor and service discovery. Give the headline and actions a solid color field, frame the existing care conversation separately, and put both searches ahead of the photograph on phones. Keep one prominent appointment action and a quieter visit guide link.

The original is a strong visual starting point. Its weakness is responsive task placement, especially the photograph between the appointment action and discovery on phones. The revised design makes that sequence deliberate without adding client JavaScript or media requests.

Who this serves

The working audience is patients and family members choosing a doctor, understanding services, requesting an appointment, or preparing to arrive. A second audience needs emergency information immediately. These priorities come from the existing site journeys and repository research; traffic analytics, interviews, device distribution, and conversion results were not supplied.

Hallelujah-specific material includes the General Hospital identity; the open-arms emblem; the established dark blue and pale aqua palette; local English and Ethiopic typography; Ethiopian care-scene artwork; separate doctor and service directories; doctor language information; request-not-confirmed-booking semantics; and the current absence of verified contact details and hospital photography. These are a better basis for design than borrowing another institution’s claims or facilities.

Concept: Hallelujah’s open-arms identity becomes a frame for a listening conversation, beside a clear route into care. The interaction is real directory search, using existing records. This is a practical expression of the brand, rather than a claim that the visual concept is unique among hospitals.

What the baseline actually does

  • Retain: the short, two-line “Care begins with you” headline, hospital name, warm conversation, original emblem, self-hosted type, high-contrast appointment button, honest image disclosure, and emergency route in the shared header.
  • Improve: replace the abstract supporting line with a sentence explaining the available patient tasks. Give service discovery a named search and browse route alongside doctors.
  • Remove: the gradient scrim and image/text overlap; the photograph interrupting the mobile action sequence; the asymmetry between a prominent doctor finder and a secondary service link.
  • Keep the useful lower-page content: the existing navigator explains routes to visitors who need guidance; the hero now supports people ready to act directly.

The original passed the automated visual inspector with zero failures. That does not make it the strongest design: at 768px, its supporting copy reaches onto the clinician; at 390px, only the doctor finder fits near the bottom of the first screen and the service route follows below. These are visible composition findings, rather than inferred conversion problems.

What the benchmarks teach

“Best” here means strong references for a particular problem, not an independently established ranking of website quality. Current first-party pages were reviewed alongside the repository’s earlier hospital screenshots. Cleveland Clinic, Aga Khan Nairobi, and Apple were also captured and visually inspected in Chromium. Four Seasons returned an access-denied page in that browser, so its current visual design was not inferred from the failed capture.

That supports keeping essential patient choices persistent; it does not establish a universal conversion penalty. Comparison and decision

Scores are structured expert judgment, not experiment results. Each criterion uses 1–5, where 5 is strongest. Weights: patient tasks 30%, readability and accessibility 25%, emotional connection 20%, delivery and resilience 15%, current asset readiness 10%. The total is the weighted score multiplied by 20. The weights prioritize the care journeys already present in this repository.

The winner is narrow. The improved full-background alternative would fix much of the original’s task ordering and has greater visual continuity. The split wins because text contrast remains independent of the image, both faces keep their own space at tablet width, and replacement imagery can vary without rebuilding a scrim. A one-point judgment difference is not statistical evidence. If emotional immersion becomes the dominant objective and authentic imagery is available, the full-background alternative deserves another test.

Search-first and text-only approaches are strong for fast access, but weaker introductions to the human care relationship. Illustration offers excellent controllability and honest brand expression; the existing conversation conveys listening more directly. Video would become more credible with consented footage of actual care or the hospital, a good poster, and voluntary playback. Campaign slides and scroll-controlled sequences add navigation and motion costs to a task that should remain immediate.

What was implemented

  1. Hierarchy: two-line headline, plain hospital identification and task description, primary appointment request, secondary visit guide, then explicit discovery. “Request” remains accurate to the existing journey.
  2. Media: retain the bundled WebP, crop around the two participants, separate it from copy, and remove the gradient. The brief entrance animates only the image; it never delays text or actions and respects reduced motion.
  3. Discovery: two independent, named search forms submit native GET queries to the existing directories. Each has a persistent label and a “View all” route for people who prefer browsing. Empty queries also browse the directory. Existing search semantics and result handling remain authoritative.
  4. Phones: order copy, appointment and visit actions, both searches, then media. The DOM follows that same reading and keyboard sequence; desktop uses a grid to put the photograph alongside the copy.
  5. Interaction: use familiar links and search buttons, visible focus, at least 44px action targets, Enter submission, and distinct accessible search landmarks. No tabs, hover-only controls, live triage, automatic suggestions, or new client bundle are needed.
  6. Urgent and practical access: retain the shared emergency route, language control, and contact/directions entry. A working call action needs verified hospital contact data; that data was not invented for this revision.

The supporting sentence was written before markup: “Hallelujah General Hospital. Find a doctor, explore services, and take your next step in care.” The hero claim is a clear route to care, proven by working directory and appointment destinations. This is a section revision within an established design, so the design skill’s existing-direction workflow was used rather than drawing a new site-wide style.

Validation and limits

Checked in Chromium at 320, 390, 600, 768, 1024, and 1440 CSS pixels: no horizontal overflow, two-line hero heading, and at least 44px action targets. Native Enter submission reaches matching doctor and service records with JavaScript enabled and disabled. Empty search, separate accessibility-tree landmarks, keyboard focus, appointment navigation, visit anchor, blocked-image fallback, reduced motion, and forced colors were also checked. This is browser and accessibility-tree verification, not a screen-reader user study.

The production build, TypeScript check, changed-component lint, directory contract checks (18 clinician and 22 service cases), and whitespace diff check passed. The repository-wide format command reports existing missing-newline and whitespace problems in prior generated reports and the bundled Lato license. Those unrelated files were left intact; changed source and this report were checked separately.

Final visual inspector: zero failures with one documented exception. Its position-based “eyebrow” heuristic mistakes the image’s semantic figcaption for a label introducing the service finder below it. That disclosure is retained and the eyebrow rule is explicitly allowed for this run. Three other warnings are retained: existing lower-page repeated panels, the established site-wide type scale, and absence of video. None justifies expanding a hero revision into a site redesign or adding unrelated footage.

Evidence: interaction measurements, baseline inspector report, final inspector report, and tablet captures before / after. Exact first-screen visibility varies with viewport height, browser chrome, zoom, text size, and future translated copy.

Implementation scope: the existing development homepage component at /. The repository uses a separate governed production template and withholds the preview directories outside development; those boundaries remain in place. This work does not publish or deploy the preview.

Next evidence to collect: test five-second orientation and doctor, service, appointment, and urgent-route tasks with patients and family members, including Amharic readers and people using constrained connections. Compare completion, first-click accuracy, time to a useful directory result, zero-result recovery, and comprehension of appointment requests. Obtain consented hospital photography when available. Do not claim a conversion lift from this expert review alone.
